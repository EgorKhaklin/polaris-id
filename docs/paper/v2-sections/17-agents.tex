\section{AI agents, proof of personhood and the future internet}
\label{sec:agents}

Nothing in this section is solved. It is the environment Polaris is preparing for, stated as a
problem, with what exists today marked apart from what would have to be built.

\subsection{The problem that is arriving}

When software agents can generate unlimited accounts, content, transactions, messages and apparent
participants, the cheap signal that a request came from a person stops being available for free.
Many digital systems will need to distinguish, without learning more than they must, between one
accountable human; one authorized agent acting for that human or for an institution; anonymous
machine activity; duplicated or synthetic identities; and organisational agents acting under an
institution's authority. Every naive way of obtaining that signal leaks identity: accounts, phone
numbers, payment instruments, biometrics. The identity question changes shape. It is no longer only
\emph{who are you}, but \emph{what kind of actor are you, whose authority are you exercising, what
are you allowed to do, and how can I verify all of that without learning anything else}. That is
the future-facing question this paper considers the most important one Polaris's core could be
extended to answer.

\subsection{What exists today that points this way}

Three ingredients of a privacy-preserving proof of personhood already run.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Uniqueness at issuance.} C3, one active token per person, is a partial unique index
    in the database. That is the root of Sybil resistance: a human can hold one active credential, and
    no application code can bypass the constraint. \sImpl
  \item \textbf{Membership without identity.} The epoch membership proof proves that a valid token
    exists in an authority's committed set with public inputs of only the root, the epoch, a context
    and a nonce; a zero-knowledge verification records no token at all; the anonymity set is a full
    epoch. \sImpl
  \item \textbf{Revocation without naming.} A revoked token drops out of the next epoch root, so a
    membership proof expires with the epoch and no relying party learns who was revoked. \sImpl
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What is missing, precisely}

Polaris is not a proof-of-personhood system today, and the gap is specific. The membership proof
stops replay of one proof, but it does not stop the same person from producing a thousand fresh proofs
to the same site and registering a thousand accounts. \emph{One human, once, here} needs a scoped
nullifier: one more public input, a hash of the holder's secret, the relying party's scope and the
epoch, so that a site can refuse a second proof from the same person while two sites cannot link
their nullifiers. The leaf derivation already mixes in a context, which is part of the way there;
exposing a nullifier is a circuit change, small and well bounded, and not built. \sFut

Two more gaps sit outside the circuit. The prover runs on the Polaris host, and a personhood
credential needs the holder to prove on their own device from a Merkle path obtained from the
published tree. And the presentation layer is full-credential: a stable value shown to every
verifier is the correlator that defeats the purpose. The pairwise, blinded and one-time presentation
technologies that would remove it are surveyed in Section~\ref{sec:research}; none is
implemented. \sFut

Two conditions the vocation imposes on any future version deserve stating before the design. The
nullifier's scope must be per relying party and per epoch, never global, because a global nullifier
is a persistent identifier and therefore the surveillance outcome the constitution refuses. And a
relying party must not be able to demand full disclosure as a precaution; the broker's registered
policy already lets a party be held to the zero-knowledge level, and that has to remain the default
posture of any personhood extension.

\subsection{Delegating authority to an agent}

\figfile{delegation}{A proposed delegation chain. The human holds the foundational credential,
which exists. Everything else is dashed: a scoped grant signed by a holder-side key, an agent that
acts only within it, and a service that verifies the chain offline and learns nothing beyond the five
answers listed. The repository has no holder-side key, no pairwise handle and no grant-scoped
revocation; each is named as an open problem.}{fig:delegation}

The relationship this paper proposes is a chain. A human possesses a foundational credential. The
human grants a narrowly scoped cryptographic authority to an agent: named actions, limits, an
expiry, a revocation handle. The agent can perform only the specified actions. A service verifies,
offline, that the agent's grant is genuine and unexpired; that it was issued by a valid principal,
without learning who; that this exact action is inside the delegated scope; that the grant has not
been revoked, without the human's own credential being revoked; and it learns nothing more than
those answers. Revoking an agent's authority must not destroy the human's underlying identity, which
is why the grant needs its own revocation path, separate from the credential's.

Polaris's existing machinery maps onto the chain more closely than one might expect. The grant is a
signed statement in the same canonical discipline as every other artifact; the service is a relying
party with the detached verifier; the expiry and revocation handle are the status assertion and
revocation feed applied to a grant rather than a credential; and the trust graph decides which
principals a service accepts. What the chain needs and the repository lacks is a holder-side key,
since the model is issuer-centric on purpose and the holder holds a credential rather than a key
pair. That is the same missing piece that blocks the browser bridge, and the repository records it as
a future credential extension. \sFut

\subsection{The future internet, concretely}

The term Web3 is used here in one narrow sense, and it has nothing to do with currency: an internet
in which people, autonomous agents, institutions, wallets, distributed services and independently
operated networks need portable cryptographic trust that does not route through one company or one
state. Against that environment the following table marks what Polaris could provide and what each
item's status is today.
