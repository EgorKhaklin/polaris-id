\section{What Polaris refuses to become}
\label{sec:boundaries}

\motif{Privacy is not freedom from coercion.}

Powerful identity infrastructure carries risks that no amount of correctness removes, because they
are risks of what a correct system can be used for. The repository names these as design and
governance constraints rather than as politics, and this section states them the same way. Each is
a way a working Polaris could be turned into something its constitution forbids, and each is stated
no more strongly than the evidence supports.

\begin{description}
  \item[Identity must not become money.] A credential that carries spending authority inherits
    programmability, and every constraint anyone wants placed on spending accretes onto the identity
    token, until the system can be told whom to refuse at a fuel pump. C10 forbids a monetary claim
    in the schema; if a value system is ever built, it lives in a separate database that consumes
    verification proofs over a boundary, and the boundary is what is load-bearing. This is the single
    most consequential architectural decision in the repository, and it is the one that never
    expires with any phase.
  \item[Privacy is not merely secrecy.] A system can know very little about a person and still be
    coercive, if every doorway requires its authorization. The zero-knowledge default bounds what is
    recorded; it does not bound what a society demands. The tiered-enrolment layer exists for this:
    exemption is a first-class, one-row state, the not-enrolled cannot be enumerated by an accident
    of schema, and a person with no documents can be enrolled through a referee whose vouching leaves
    no mark on the credential. The coexistence plan names the sunset of the old credential as the
    moment the new one becomes compulsory, and refuses to compute that verdict from what the issuer
    can see. The repository's own threat model names the strongest attack on this layer as making
    non-enrolment civically impossible from outside, no token, no healthcare, and says plainly that
    the schema cannot stop it, only make it a named act.
  \item[Anti-coercion is a vocation, not a claim.] The vocation above the ten constraints says no
    person may be compelled to surrender their identity. The mechanism that serves it, the duress
    code, resists the coercer it was built for and not the two others the operating contract named,
    and against lawful or institutional access its indelible record is evidence against the holder
    (Section~\ref{sec:core}). So the front door says \emph{duress-aware} and a check refuses
    \emph{compulsion-resistant}. The boundary is the honest one: the system notices duress; it does
    not resist compulsion, and a vocation that pointed at the adversary the mechanism is weakest
    against would have been the opposite of what the word promised.
  \item[Technical federation is not social decentralisation.] Polaris is federated in code: each
    authority its own key, no central store, no transitive trust, and an operator
    bound to one authority even inside a shared instance. Two instances run by one author on one
    machine demonstrate the protocol and nothing about who runs it. Whether authorities, witnesses
    and relying parties are in fact independent is a property of a deployment, not of the repository,
    and this paper marks every such claim as requiring external validation.
  \item[Revocation must not become civil erasure.] A revoked token is a terminal state in an
    append-only record, not a deletion; lost tokens stay lost, and their data is not erased.
    Succession is by reference. Mass revocation is bounded by a ceiling and a co-signer, and the
    ceiling itself can only be changed with a reason on record. Erasure, when a jurisdiction requires
    it, is pseudonymisation with an append-only record of who, when and why, never of the prior name;
    a pilot's wind-down is that erasure, executed rather than promised. An authority can end a
    person's credential; the schema refuses to let it end the person's history.
  \item[Relying-party correlation is different from issuer unlinkability.] The issuer's database
    cannot rebuild the zero-knowledge verification graph. Two relying parties can join their logs by
    whatever stable material a presentation showed them. Both sentences are true at once, and Version
    3 can say how much of the second was measured: on the native zero-knowledge path a colluding
    pair has no advantage beyond the anonymity set against an adversary that matches on equality,
    and nothing floors that set above one; on the interoperable path a single presentation leaves
    nine issuer-signed values identical at every verifier (Section~\ref{sec:privacy}). A reader who
    hears \emph{unlinkable} and imagines either path is stronger than that has been misled, which is
    why the repository states the boundary positively and the verifier reports which kind of
    presentation it was shown.
  \item[Interoperating imports the ecosystem's properties.] Speaking the format an outside wallet
    speaks was the first thing anyone outside used, and it carried the format's linkability with it.
    The repository records that as a cost of interoperating, not a reason to stop, and it does not
    let the interoperable path borrow the native path's privacy claims.
  \item[No population graph.] The Atlas is bounded and never locates a zero-knowledge event; Athena
    is forbidden a person node, a stable subject surrogate and a subject traversal; the schema carries
    no attribute to filter a population by; quotas and alerts are keyed by agency, never by person;
    the transparency report carries no total a small count could be recovered from. The refusal is
    structural because a population graph, once built, outlives the intentions of whoever built it.
  \item[Machine learning must not turn identity infrastructure into scoring.] The proposed learning
    layer may learn how the identity system behaves and must never learn how people behave. Fraud
    or risk scores for individuals, behavioural profiles, relationship inference, eligibility or
    likelihood-of-revocation scoring, person-level clustering or embeddings, and model-driven denial
    of rights are presumptively prohibited, and a recommendation is evidence, never authority.
\end{description}

The repository also lists permanent non-goals that do not expire with any roadmap phase: no payment
rails, no central biometric database, no population-scale attribute filtering or analytics, no social
scoring, no commercial or advertising use of verification data, and no real personal data before a
consent framework exists. Every one of them is a way the system could be made more useful to someone,
and every one is refused on sight. Since the operating contract, one more refusal governs the
repository itself: no new capability is built because it would be elegant, complete or desirable; a
product change needs a named outside party that requires it, or an executable counterexample against
something already promised.

\analogy{The laws above the town bind the town. The hall may not mint coin. The register may not
be turned into a list of who is absent. The gates may not be closed on a neighbour on the word of a
neighbour's neighbour. A name struck from the register stays legible as struck. No clerk, however
clever, may be set to guessing which townspeople are likely to cause trouble. And the town does not
claim that its walls keep a person safe from a guard who is standing beside them.}
